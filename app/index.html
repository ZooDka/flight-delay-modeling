Timing is everything.

The same route is a different product at 7am than at 7pm. Delays pile up through the day as aircraft and crews fall behind, so an early departure is the single most reliable choice you can make.

Mean departure delay by scheduled hour across 336,776 real flights. The afternoon window runs roughly five to seven times the delay of the early morning bank.

How it works

Data. Each lookup fetches the flight's recent departure-delay statistics from AeroDataBox through a key-safe edge proxy: the full P5 to P95 percentile curve, observed delay brackets, sample size, and the arrival-side curve when available.

Model. Delay is modeled as a mixture: an on-time cluster plus a log-normal late tail. The family was chosen by fitting four candidates to 336,776 real flights and comparing AIC and Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics, then validating on held-out data. Per flight, the mixture is calibrated to the fetched statistics, and a seeded Monte-Carlo simulation produces the distribution, percentiles, and exceedance probabilities.

Fit check. When observed percentiles are available, the dashboard reports the maximum gap between the model CDF and the observed curve, a KS-style statistic, so you can see how faithful the model is before trusting the tail it extrapolates.

Pricing. A parametric delay product pays a fixed amount when delay exceeds a trigger. Expected loss per policy is the trigger probability times the payout; the fair premium is that loss divided by the target loss ratio; and a binomial portfolio simulation turns per-policy risk into season-level expected payouts and a 1-in-20 adverse scenario, the number a risk team would hold capital against.

Honest limits. Tracker windows are recent and seasonal, so numbers move with the season. Policies on the same flight correlate on disrupted days, which fattens portfolio tails beyond the independence assumption shown here. Both are stated on the dashboard rather than hidden.
